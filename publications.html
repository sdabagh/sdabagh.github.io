Research

Research Interests

I study student outcomes in community college mathematics and statistics. Two threads run through the work: what happened to course success and equity gaps when California placed students directly into transfer level statistics under AB 705 and AB 1705, and how AI assisted course and assessment design changes the cognitive demand of what we ask students and the distribution of their results. I also apply the same quantitative methods to questions in public health and social policy. Every study is reported with its limitations stated plainly.

Keywords: community college mathematics, placement reform, educational equity, AI assisted course design, assessment, quantitative methods

Manuscripts

Rising Success, Unresolved Gaps: Student-Level Statistics Outcomes Across California's Community College Placement-Reform Era, 2018 to 2025

Author: Safaa Dabagh

Journal: Journal of Postsecondary Student Success

Status: Under Review

A student level analysis of 867 enrollments in one instructor's transfer level introductory statistics sections across 17 terms (Fall 2018 to Fall 2025) at a large California community college, spanning the AB 705 and AB 1705 placement reforms. In Fall and Spring terms, 42% of students passed before AB 705 and 65% under AB 1705, and withdrawals fell from about one in five to one in forty. Observed success rose in every racial and ethnic group, clearly for Latinx and Black students. The gap between White and Latinx students went from 25 to 24 points, but the study could only have detected a narrowing larger than the whole gap. The paper describes the rise; it does not attribute it to the reforms.

AI-Assisted Assessment Design in Mathematics and Statistics: A Multi-Course Bloom's Taxonomy Analysis of Cognitive Demand Shift

Author: Safaa Dabagh

Journal: Teaching Statistics

Status: Revise & Resubmit

A Bloom's taxonomy analysis of how AI assisted assessment design shifts the cognitive demand of mathematics and statistics course materials. Assessment items across multiple courses are coded by cognitive level to characterize where AI supported design raises or lowers the demand placed on students, informing how instructors can use AI tools without eroding the rigor of their assessments.

Preprints

Raising the Floor or Changing the Test? AI-Assisted Course Design and Student Outcomes in Two Community College Mathematics Courses

Author: Safaa Dabagh

Repository: EdArXiv / OSF preprint (earlier version, titled Raising the Floor, Holding the Bar)

DOI: 10.35542/osf.io/x9nkw_v1

Journal: Teaching Mathematics and its Applications

Status: Under Review

A practitioner case study of four sections of two community college mathematics courses (College Algebra for STEM and Introductory Statistics), each taught before and after an AI assisted redesign. Among students who took the algebra final, scores rose from 61.8% to 78.7%, most at the bottom of the distribution, but the exam also changed from free response to multiple choice; with guessing points removed, the gain shrinks and is no longer clear. Statistics held steady on a harder exam, and quizzes built together with the exams kept a link to the final while question bank quizzes showed almost none. The revised PDF is the current version.

Economic Disadvantage and Mass Shootings Across U.S. States, 2018 to 2024: A State-Level Panel Analysis

Author: Safaa Dabagh

Repository: Research Square preprint (updated October 2026)

Journal: Injury Epidemiology

Status: Under Review

Mass shootings in the United States roughly doubled after 2019 and stayed high. Using every Gun Violence Archive mass shooting in the 50 states from 2018 to 2024 (3,783 incidents) and state data drawn directly from official sources, this study finds that where poverty and economic disadvantage concentrate, mass shootings concentrate too: each percentage point of poverty is associated with about 15% more mass shootings per million residents, and the 10 highest poverty states have 1.7 times the rate of the 10 lowest. Poverty cannot be separated from low income or segregation at the state level, so the finding is about economic disadvantage as a whole. State data cannot settle whether gun laws work, and state mental illness rates are not a guide to where shootings happen. These are associations, not proof of cause. Updated October 2026: the state data were rebuilt from official files and the conclusions revised; one script reproduces every number.
